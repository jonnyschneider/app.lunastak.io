Architects work from early, estimated measurements and describe configuration, drawer counts, shadow gaps and kicker heights, but not manufacture-ready detail. The cabinet maker is the first person in the whole process who can produce a real number, and they only show up at pricing. That timing produces a predictable failure sequence: the design gets priced, it's unaffordable, the architect gets blamed, the client shops it around, discovers the design itself is the expensive part, and everyone ends up doing unpaid rework to save the job.

This isn't a one-off failure, it's structural. Joinery can be a quarter of a $250k renovation, one of the biggest line items in the build, yet builders carry it as a provisional sum priced off early drawings because joiners aren't part of their crew the way framers or electricians are. Cabinet makers sit near the end of the schedule, so they absorb all the delay that piles up before them, and the value-engineering work they do to save a job is usually unpaid. Jobs shrink, get deferred, or die after that work is already done.

The opportunity is to move the cabinet maker's design-and-feasibility judgment earlier in the process, before pricing, without asking anyone to commit.
